Reach your home server, NAS or office PC from anywhere

Mesh Town links your own devices directly to each other. Open the web panel of a NAS, SSH into a home server or connect to a work machine by remote desktop from any network — without opening a single port on your router.

  • No port forwarding
  • No public IP needed
  • No central server or accounts
  • Survives a change of network
Install Mesh Town See how it works

Free and open source. For macOS, Windows, Linux, Android and OpenWrt or Keenetic routers. Early release: it works, but it is not a hardened product yet.

Sound familiar?

  • The NAS panel, away from home

    You want the web panel of your NAS or Home Assistant while you are out, and nothing on your router is open for it.

    You open: http://nas.meshtown

  • No public IP at home

    Your provider puts you behind its own NAT, so port forwarding is not even an option. Mesh Town does not need it.

  • A computer at your parents'

    You look after a machine at a relative's or a client's place, behind a router you do not control.

    You type: ssh -p 2022 user@127.0.0.1

  • The work PC from home

    Remote desktop to the office machine, with nothing opened on the office router.

    You connect to: 127.0.0.1:5389

  • A tool for a few colleagues

    A dev database or an internal web tool, shared only with the people you pick — no cloud service and no server to look after.

    Shared with: Selected members

  • A service that trusts one address

    A service accepts connections only from your office IP. Point a browser at Mesh Town, and it leaves through a member's node at the office.

    Proxy: socks5h://127.0.0.1:1080

Not for you if you need

  • a whole network joined: Mesh Town shares ports of programs, it adds no virtual network adapter;
  • it on an iPhone or iPad;
  • anonymity: you trust the members you invite, and an exit sees which hosts you connect to;
  • a hardened product today: this is an early release.

Install in 3 steps

Pick your system.

macOS

  1. Install:

    Homebrew

    brew install mesh-town/macos/meshtown
meshtown

    App

    Download for Apple silicon or Intel, unzip and move MeshTown.app to Applications. The app is not notarized: on the first launch press Open Anyway in System Settings → Privacy & Security.

  2. Start Mesh Town, open the node page from the menu bar icon and set a password.
  3. Press Create a new network, or Join with an invite from a member. To add the next device, press Invite a member: it shows a link and a QR code.
Open shared web panels by name

In your system's proxy settings, turn on automatic proxy configuration (PAC) and paste http://127.0.0.1:1080/proxy.pac. A shared panel then opens at http://<device-name>.meshtown. For other programs, press Open on this computer next to a shared port on the node page.

Under the hood

The short version for specialists. Open any line for the details.

Discovery: an encrypted record in the public DHT

Every node of a network knows the network key. From it the node derives a place in the public DHT (HyperDHT, a distributed directory nobody owns) and publishes a small record there about once a minute: the list of member nodes, encrypted with ChaCha20-Poly1305. Without the key, one sees only a public signing key, a salt and ciphertext.

Connection: direct NAT hole punching; relay only through your own member

Nodes connect by NAT hole punching, so home routers and most NATs let the connection through with no port opened. If that fails, a member of the same network with a public address relays the connection; nobody outside the network does. A network where every device is behind NAT has no such member, and some mobile operators prevent direct connections.

Encryption: UDX streams with Noise, plus a handshake on the network secret

Each connection is an encrypted stream: UDX is a reliable transport over UDP, Noise a well-studied framework for encrypted, authenticated connections. On top of it the nodes run a handshake that checks both sides know the network secret; a node that fails it is disconnected. A stranger who learns a node's id gets an answer only from the sign-in and invite services.

Membership: a private key, administrators and single-use invites

A network is a key you keep private: a label you choose plus a random part of about 130 bits. Whoever creates the network is its first administrator, and only administrators invite. An invite is a single-use string, valid for a day, that does not contain the network key. One node can be in up to 8 networks. The node page shows an invite as a link and a QR code (Invite a member); on Android, Scan QR reads it.

Names: http://<device-name>.meshtown through the node's proxy

A shared web interface is at http://<device-name>.meshtown. The device name is folded to lowercase ASCII letters, digits and dashes — "Café Router" becomes cafe-router — and two devices with the same name get a short suffix from their id. A port other than 80 is written as usual: http://nas.meshtown:8080. Names work only through the node's proxy, and only over http://.

Programs: a SOCKS5 and HTTP proxy on 127.0.0.1:1080, or a local port

Programs reach the network through the node's proxy at 127.0.0.1:1080 (SOCKS5, a standard proxy protocol, and HTTP on one port), or through a local port: Open on this computer turns a member's port 22 into 127.0.0.1:2022. A device shares ports of programs running on it, TCP or UDP, with all members or selected ones. The node's own page is at http://127.0.0.1:7777: plain HTTP, reachable from your local network unless you run meshtown access localhost. Never share the page port.

Exit through a member: one hop, chosen from your list; the exit sees hostnames

A member can also agree to be an exit: the others then send traffic out through its internet connection via the proxy, for example to reach a service that accepts connections only from your office IP. The node uses the exits from a list you set, in order. The exit sees which hosts you connect to.

Limits: ports, not networks; UDP over reliable streams; no iPhone app
  • Ports, not networks. Mesh Town connects TCP and UDP ports of programs on the device itself. There is no virtual network interface, no subnet routing and no capture of all traffic.
  • Programs use the proxy. A program has to work through a SOCKS5 or HTTP proxy, or connect to a local port on 127.0.0.1.
  • UDP rides on reliable streams. Order is kept, but a stalled link delays everything behind it, and a datagram larger than 2048 bytes is truncated.
  • Trust, not anonymity. Members you invite are trusted, and an exit sees which hosts you connect to. There is no iPhone or iPad app.
  • Early software. Releases of the 0.3.x line are a working network node, not a hardened product.

Questions

Does it route all my traffic?

No. Mesh Town connects ports of programs: a program reaches the network through the node's proxy or a local port. There is no virtual network interface, and other traffic is not touched.

What if two devices cannot connect directly?

A member of your network with a public address relays between them. With no such member they stay unconnected. Behind home routers and most NATs a direct connection works; some mobile operators prevent it.

Who can reach the services I share?

Only members of your network, and for each port you choose: all members or selected ones. A port is shared only after you publish it.

How does the browser setting behave?

The address http://127.0.0.1:1080/proxy.pac is an automatic proxy configuration (PAC) file served by the node. Ordinary sites go through the node, with a fallback to a direct connection while Mesh Town is stopped; *.meshtown addresses always go through the node, and local addresses go direct. Strict mode (Settings → proxy) removes the direct fallback, so a site never opens outside the chosen exit.

Can I share my network key instead of invites?

Better not. The key and the settings file are secret. Invite people with single-use invites, and hand them over in person.

Is it free and open source?

Yes, under AGPL-3.0-only. Peer discovery and transport come from townjump, a Rust port of Holesail.
